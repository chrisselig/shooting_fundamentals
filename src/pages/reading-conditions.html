03

The Environment As A Speed Gauge

Naked Eye

If the match director hasn't placed wind flags along the range, use these natural baselines to estimate speed.

2–3MPH Wind is faintly felt on your face; leaves on trees may twitch slightly.
4–6MPH Small twigs and light grass move continuously. Match flags stand out at roughly a 45-degree angle.
8–12MPH Whole branches of bushes shake, loose dust and paper lift off the ground, and flags blow straight out horizontally.
04

Build A Wind Bracket Before Your Stage

Squad Line

Never try to guess the exact mile-per-hour number while your match timer is ticking. Establish a bracket while waiting in the squad line.

  1. Observe the flags for 2 minutes before your turn.
  2. Identify the lull — the lowest the wind drops, e.g. 4 mph — and the gust — the highest it kicks up, e.g. 10 mph.
  3. Find your holds for those two exact speeds — see Kestrel 5700 Elite and AB Quantum for how to generate them, or use a printed cheat sheet like Cole-TAC's.
  4. Write both holds down before you build your position.
On the clock, it's one decision Lull hold or gust hold — not a fresh wind call for every shot. Deciding between exactly two numbers, already written down, is fast. Re-reading the wind from scratch under a shot clock is not.
05

Turning A Bracket Into A Hold

Application

Reading the wind only matters if you can turn it into a number on the reticle. Two things determine that number: how much of the wind's speed is actually hitting you side-on (its value), and how far the bullet has to fly through it.

The Wind Clock

Picture a clock face laid flat over the range, 12 pointing downrange at the target and 6 back at you. Where the wind is blowing from on that clock sets how much of it actually pushes your bullet sideways:

Clock positionValueMultiplier
12 or 6 (head/tail wind)No value×0
1, 5, 7, 11 o'clockHalf value×0.5
3 or 9 o'clockFull value×1.0

That three-tier version (no/half/full) is the one to actually use under a shot clock — it's fast and it's what most wind-doping instruction teaches. If you want a fourth in-between step, the diagonals at 1:30/4:30/7:30/10:30 run closer to three-quarter value (×0.75) than half, but the extra precision rarely earns its keep when you're calling wind by eye.

A widely-shared "wind clock" gets this backwards A version circulating online (including in AI-generated answers) labels 2/4/8/10 o'clock as half value and 1/5/7/11 o'clock as quarter value. That's swapped from what long-range wind-doping instruction actually teaches, and the underlying angle math doesn't support a quarter-value tier at 1/5/7/11 in the first place. Use the table above.

Multiply by your wind clock value, and scale roughly linearly for other wind speeds (5 mph ≈ half this hold, 15 mph ≈ one-and-a-half times it — that part of the physics holds up well).

This table is a starting point, not your data Change the bullet, the muzzle velocity, or the altitude and every number here moves — a faster supersonic-loaded .22LR gave meaningfully different holds than the subsonic load above when checked against the same crosswind. Treat this as roughly what to expect walking onto a stage without a meter, then build your real numbers in Kestrel 5700 Elite or AB Quantum and confirm them with truing against actual impacts. A Mildot Master covers the same ground as a battery-free backup. 300–400 m is included for completeness, not because it's a realistic NRL22 distance — at that range a subsonic .22LR has dropped so far (elevation holds well past 20 MIL from a 100-yard zero) that most scopes run out of adjustment before wind is even the limiting problem.

Two claims worth naming and correcting, since they get repeated: wind drift does not scale with distance squared — going from 100 to 200 yards roughly doubles the hold, not quadruples it, because the angular MIL value divides the growing drift by the growing range. And there's no separately-named "wind formula shortcut" specific to rimfire circulating under any particular person's name — the generic range-times-wind-speed-over-a-constant shortcut long-range shooters use for centerfire doesn't have a verified constant for slow, rapidly-decelerating rimfire bullets, which is exactly why the table above comes from a full trajectory calculation instead of a mental-math shortcut.

06

Your Gun Number

Personal Constant

The centerfire long-range crowd carries a single "gun number" — a constant you divide into range×wind to get a mental-math hold, no app or table needed. The caution above still applies: there is no verified rimfire version of that constant you can borrow. But you can build your own from data you already trued, and it's accurate enough for a fast gut-check at the distances NRL22 actually shoots.

Yours, not universal This number is derived from one load, one zero, one altitude — your own. Change the bullet, the muzzle velocity, or move to a very different elevation and it stops being true. It is not the subsonic reference load's constant, and it is not transferable to someone else's rifle.

The Formula

Solved for the one input you don't already have — the constant — using a full-value (3/9 o'clock) hold you've already trued at one reference distance:

Gun Number = (Range ÷ 100) × Wind(mph) × Value ÷ Hold(MIL)

Once you have it, run the formula the other direction on the clock: Hold(MIL) = (Range ÷ 100) × Wind(mph) × Value ÷ Gun Number. Range is in yards (hundreds of yards × wind × value), because that's the unit your reticle's MIL subtension math assumes — a metric gun number needs its own truing pass in meters, not a unit conversion of this one.

Deriving It

  1. Pick the one distance you shoot most — 100 yards is typical for NRL22 — and get a trued, full-value, known-speed wind hold there (from Kestrel 5700 Elite, AB Quantum, or your own logged impacts).
  2. Plug range, wind speed, value (1.0 for full value), and that trued hold into the formula above.
  3. Round to one decimal. That's your gun number for this load, this zero, this elevation.
  4. Check it against a second distance you've also trued. If it's off by more than a click or two, true that distance separately rather than trusting the constant across the board — see the worked check below for why.

Worked Example

Using this page's own reference load and its trued 100-yard hold from the table in section 05 — 10 mph, full value, 1.2 MIL:

(100 ÷ 100) × 10 × 1.0 ÷ 1.2 = 8.3

The drift grows with distance because a single constant can't fully capture a rimfire bullet's non-linear deceleration — the same reason section 05 above has no shortcut for a universal version of this number.

Adjusting For Fluctuating FPS

.22LR velocity swings lot-to-lot — 30–80 fps isn't unusual, and cold ammo can shoot meaningfully slower than warm. A slower bullet spends longer in the wind, so it needs more hold than your reference gun number predicts; a faster lot needs less. Scale the gun number itself, in direct proportion to the fps change:

Adjusted Gun Number = Gun Number × (Actual FPS ÷ Reference FPS)
Linear scaling, not a new trajectory This is a straight-line approximation around your reference velocity — good enough for the kind of lot-to-lot or cold-morning swing you'll actually see (tens of fps), not a substitute for re-running the real math. If a change is large (new bullet weight, a different box entirely, 100+ fps off), true a fresh reference hold in Kestrel 5700 Elite or AB Quantum instead of trusting the scale-up.
