Section 02

The Fundamentals

Seven pieces that stack on top of each other. Trigger control is the one that matters most for a new shooter's group size — but each of the others is what lets trigger control actually work.

01

Stance & Natural Point of Aim

The goal of stance isn't a textbook shape — it's getting your skeleton, not your muscles, to hold the rifle on target. Muscle tires and drifts over a string of fire; bone doesn't.

Natural point of aim (NPA)

NPA is where the rifle settles when you're relaxed — not where you're actively steering it. If your NPA isn't on target, you'll fight your own muscles for the whole shot, and your group will drift the direction your muscles fatigue toward.

Adjust your base, not your arms If the crosshair lands off-target, don't muscle it back with your shoulders or arms — that just creates tension you'll fight for the rest of the string. Move your hips, feet, or the position of your bag/bipod instead, then re-test.

Off a bag or bipod (the common NRL22 setup), NPA is mostly about hip and shoulder alignment behind the rifle plus consistent bag placement — see Positional Shooting for how this changes at a barricade.

02

Grip & Rifle Hold

Grip should be firm and consistent, not a death-grip. The goal is repeatability — the exact same hold, shot after shot — not white knuckles.

  • Firing hand: consistent, moderate pressure straight back into the stock. A relaxed hand lets your trigger finger move independently; a clenched one drags the rest of the hand along with it.
  • Support hand: on a bag, the support hand typically controls the rear bag (squeeze to raise, relax to lower) rather than gripping the fore-end — this is one of the bigger habit changes coming from a bench or hunting background into NRL22-style shooting.
  • Shoulder pressure: consistent, firm contact into the shoulder pocket, every position, every shot. Inconsistent shoulder pressure is a quiet source of vertical stringing that's easy to blame on wind instead.
03

Sight Alignment vs. Sight Picture

Commonly Confused

These are two different things stacked on top of each other. Alignment is about the sight itself; picture is about the sight plus the target.

If you're shooting a scope (most NRL22 rifles) The alignment concept becomes eye relief and cheek weld: your eye positioned consistently behind the ocular lens so you see a full, round field of view with no dark crescent shadowing one edge. Sight picture is then that clean, shadow-free view with the reticle sitting on your point of aim. A vignette in the corner of your scope means your alignment is off before you even get to the picture — fix head position first, not your hold on the target.
04

Trigger Control

Biggest Lever For A New Shooter

More group size for new shooters comes from the trigger than from anything else on this page. Everything above this point exists to get you a stable, aligned platform — trigger control is what stops you from disturbing it right at the moment that matters.

Straight-back pressure

Press straight back, isolating movement to the trigger finger. Contact the trigger with the pad of your finger, not the first joint — the joint tends to pull the shot sideways as it curls, since it's not moving in a straight line relative to the bore.

Anticipation ("flinch")

The most common failure isn't a weak trigger finger, it's the rest of the hand or body bracing for the shot right before it breaks — pushing down, or clenching — because you know roughly when the shot is coming. The fix is to remove your own certainty about exactly when it'll break.

Ball-and-dummy drill Have someone load your rifle for you (or mix in a snap cap without looking), so you genuinely don't know if the next trigger press will fire. Watch the muzzle on the dummy round — any dip or jump is your anticipation, made visible.
05

Breathing & Respiratory Pause

Breathing moves the rifle. The respiratory pause is the small natural gap between breathing out and breathing back in, where things settle enough to break a shot.

  • Breathe normally on approach to the shot, don't pre-load a big breath.
  • Exhale to a natural, comfortable stop — not a forced, empty-lung stop.
  • Break the shot in that pause, within a couple of seconds. If it doesn't happen, breathe again and reset — don't fight your own oxygen debt for a "perfect" moment.
06

Follow-Through

The shot isn't over when the trigger breaks. Keep everything — grip, cheek weld, position — exactly the same through recoil and for a beat after, so you don't disturb the round on its way out and so you can actually see where it went.

Calling your shot

"Calling the shot" means knowing where the crosshair was at the moment it broke, before you see the impact. If your call matches the impact, your fundamentals are sound and any miss is likely a wind or dope problem. If your call doesn't match the impact, that's your fundamentals telling you something — usually trigger control or follow-through.

Stay in the scope Keep watching through the sight after the shot breaks instead of lifting your head to look for the hit. On a rimfire at NRL22 distances you can often see your own trace or impact through recoil if you stay put.
07

Dry Fire Practice

Dry fire — practicing the full shot process with no ammunition — is where most of a new shooter's improvement actually happens, because you get to isolate trigger control and position without recoil or noise masking what your body is doing.

Safety for dry fire Clear the firearm and physically verify the chamber is empty. Remove all ammunition from the room, not just the rifle. Always point in a genuinely safe direction with a proper backstop even though you've verified it's unloaded — rule 1 doesn't get suspended for practice.

What to practice

  • NPA setup on a target, position by position.
  • Trigger press without disturbing the sight picture — watch the reticle through the break, it should barely move.
  • Position transitions for barricades — building the position, not just the shot.
  • A .22LR-rated snap cap protects the firing pin on rimfire actions during repeated dry fire — check your specific rifle's manual, since not all rimfires tolerate sustained dry fire without one.
