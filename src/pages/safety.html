Safety

Everything else on this site assumes you already have these rules cold. They come before technique because they matter more than technique.

Why this page is first These rules exist so that a mistake at any other step — a stumble, a slipped finger, a round left in the chamber you forgot about — never turns into an injury. They are not about skill. They work even when you (or the person next to you) does something wrong.

The Four Rules

Non-negotiable

These are usually taught as a single "four rules" set. Different organizations word them slightly differently — the wording below is the common version. Learn the ideas, not just the sentences.

RULE 1

Treat every firearm as if it is loaded

Never handle a gun on the assumption that it's empty — not because someone told you it was clear, not because you personally unloaded it five minutes ago. You check the chamber yourself, every time you pick one up, and then you keep behaving as though it's loaded anyway.

In practice: when a firearm is handed to you, open the action and visually and physically check the chamber before you do anything else with it — even if the person handing it to you says "it's empty."
RULE 2

Never point the muzzle at anything you are not willing to destroy

The muzzle direction is the one thing you control at all times, in every position, whether the gun is loaded or not. "Safe direction" usually means downrange into a berm or backstop — on a walking course or at a barricade it also means: not your own foot, not your support hand, not the person next to you as you turn.

In practice: when you turn to talk to someone at the line, the muzzle turns with you or points down — it never sweeps across another person to get there.
RULE 3

Keep your finger off the trigger until your sights are on target and you've decided to shoot

Your trigger finger's resting place is straight along the frame or receiver, indexed above the trigger guard, until the exact moment you intend to fire. This is the rule that stops a startle, a stumble, or an adrenaline spike from turning into an unintended shot.

In practice: moving between positions on a stage, climbing onto a barricade, or walking with the rifle — finger comes off the trigger and stays off until you're set and looking through the sight at your target.
RULE 4

Be sure of your target and what's beyond it

A .22LR bullet can travel well over a mile. Know what you're shooting at, and know what's behind it — a proper backstop absorbs the round if you miss or if it passes through. This applies on a square range with a dirt berm and it applies just as much informally, like plinking on private land.

In practice: at a match, that's the berm or steel behind a target. Off a formal range, that means confirming there's a legitimate, adequate backstop — not just open field — behind everything you might hit.
Canadian Firearms Safety Course — ACTS & PROVE

If you took the CFSC for a PAL, this is the same idea in the mnemonic form it's taught in. ACTS is how you handle any firearm, always:

  • A — Assume every firearm is loaded
  • C — Control the muzzle direction at all times
  • T — Trigger finger off the trigger and out of the trigger guard
  • S — See that the firearm is unloaded — PROVE it safe

PROVE is the five-step check to actually confirm a firearm is unloaded, in order:

  • P — Point the firearm in the safest available direction
  • R — Remove all ammunition (magazine out, then any cartridge in hand)
  • O — Observe the chamber, visually
  • V — Verify the feeding path, physically — look and feel
  • E — Examine the bore for obstructions

Range Commands & Etiquette

Squad Line

Every range and match runs on a shared vocabulary so that everyone on the line knows the state of things without guessing. Exact phrasing varies by range officer and club — the concepts underneath don't.

Hot range / cold range

Hot range: firearms may be loaded and handled at the firing line. Cold range: all actions must be open, firearms unloaded and often flagged, and nobody handles a gun — this is when it's safe to walk downrange to check or change targets.

Common commands

  • "Line is hot" — shooting may begin, everyone stays behind the line.
  • "Cease fire" — stop immediately, finger off the trigger, muzzle stays downrange or straight up depending on range rules, wait for further instruction. Called for any safety concern, not just at the end of a string.
  • "Make ready" — at your position, load and prepare for the string of fire on the range officer's instruction.
  • "Is the line clear?" / "Clear." — the range officer confirms every shooter's action is open and firearm is safe before declaring the range cold.
When in doubt, stop and ask. No range officer or match director has ever been upset that a shooter stopped to double-check a command. Announce "cease fire" yourself if you see something unsafe — that authority isn't reserved for officials only.

Etiquette

  • Actions stay open and chamber flags in whenever you're not actively on the line shooting.
  • Ask before touching someone else's rifle or gear.
  • Announce loudly if you need to step in front of the line or handle a firearm during a cold range — and don't, until you're told it's safe to.
  • Keep the muzzle managed even in casual moments — between strings, while chatting, while your rifle is on a bag.
03

Eye & Ear Protection

Always On

Non-negotiable, every time a firearm is in use nearby — not just when you're the one shooting.

Eyes

Wear shooting glasses rated for impact (look for an ANSI Z87.1 rating) any time you or anyone near you is shooting. They protect against ejected brass, occasional case gas, and any fragment coming back off a target or steel.

Ears

.22LR is quieter than centerfire rifle, but it is still loud enough to cause cumulative hearing damage, especially indoors or suppressed only partially. Wear both plugs and muffs on louder ranges, or muffs alone outdoors at minimum.

Electronic muffs Worth the money if you can swing it — they amplify quiet range chatter and commands while clamping down hard on impulse noise from the shot itself, so you're not pulling them off between strings just to hear the range officer.

Legal & Regulatory Sources

Not Legal Advice

Firearm law varies enormously by country, state or province, and municipality — and it changes. This site will not summarize it, because a secondhand summary is exactly the kind of thing that's dangerously out of date or wrong for your jurisdiction. Go to the regulator directly.

  • Canada: RCMP Canadian Firearms Program
  • United States: ATF — Firearms, plus your state's own statutes
  • United Kingdom: Home Office firearms law guidance

These are examples of the type of official source to consult, not a claim about which one applies to you. Find your own country's, state's, or province's regulator, and if you're unsure how a law applies to your situation, that's a question for a lawyer or your local regulator — not a website.
