What It Is

No Batteries

Two flat discs, one on top of the other, that rotate against each other — a slide rule, not a computer. Made by Mildot Enterprises. No power source, so it can't die or freeze up.

02

Finding Range

Needs A Known Size

This only works if you know (or can estimate) the actual size of something on target — a steel plate, a barricade width, a torso. No known size, no range.

  1. Read the mil subtension of that known-size object through your scope's reticle — e.g. an 18" plate measures 1.5 mils tall.
  2. Line up the target's size on the outer ring against the mil reading on the inner disc.
  3. Read the range off the index window where they meet.
Your size estimate sets your accuracy ceiling Guess the target size wrong by even an inch or two and the range comes out wrong — at longer distances that error compounds fast. The tool's math is exact; your size estimate usually isn't.
03

Finding Your Hold

Drop & Wind

Once range is set on the discs from step 02, don't move them — a second scale around the edge reads out a drop or wind correction in MOA or MIL for that same range.

  1. Leave the range dialed in from the range-estimation step.
  2. Read the edge scale for the elevation (drop) correction at that range.
  3. Add a wind hold the same way, using the tool's wind scale and your called wind speed — see Turning A Bracket Into A Hold for how to call the wind itself.
It's a generic chart, not your rifle's dope The drop scale printed on the tool assumes a generic trajectory shape, not your specific bullet and muzzle velocity. Use it as a rough field backup, and build your real numbers with Kestrel 5700 Elite or AB Quantum, confirmed by truing against actual impacts.
04

Buying The Right One

Match Your Scope
  • Mil or MOA — buy the version that matches your reticle and turrets. Mixing units forces error-prone conversion under time pressure.
  • Yards or meters — confirm the slider is set to the unit you actually range in before you start; it's the most commonly reported mistake.

Sold direct at mildot.com and through most rifle-optics retailers. Worth carrying as a backup precisely because it has nothing to power on — not because it's more accurate than a trued electronic solution.
