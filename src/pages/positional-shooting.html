Section 03

Positional Shooting

NRL22 isn't bench shooting. Most of a stage is spent finding bone support on something that isn't a bench — a barrel, a ladder, a wall, a barricade — usually against a timer.

01

Why It's Different From The Bench

On a bench or prone off a bipod, the ground and the rest do most of the stabilizing. Off a barricade or prop, your body is a bigger part of the rest — and the stage designer picked that prop specifically because it isn't comfortable or natural. The fundamentals from the Fundamentals page don't change; how you build them into position does.

02

Building A Position

Same checklist every time, regardless of the specific prop:

  1. Bone, not muscle. Look for a way to stack skeleton (forearm on a knee, shoulder into a wall, elbow locked into a hip) instead of holding the rifle up with muscle tension alone.
  2. Consistent contact points. Same part of your body or bag touching the same part of the rifle or prop every time you build that position — this is what makes the position repeatable rather than a lucky one-off.
  3. Re-check NPA at the prop. Natural point of aim still applies here — see Fundamentals → NPA. At a barricade you adjust it with foot position and body angle to the prop, not by muscling the rifle over.
  4. Minimize what's touching the barrel. Contact on the barrel itself (rather than the stock, chassis, or a free-floated handguard's mounting point) shifts point of impact as pressure changes shot to shot. More on this below.
How to actually check NPA Settle into the position, close your eyes, take a couple of relaxed breaths, then open them. If the reticle drifted off target, don't pull it back with your hands — adjust your feet, hips, or body angle to the prop and re-check. Repeat until the reticle is still on target when your eyes open. Fighting the sights back with muscle is exactly what this test is meant to catch.
03

Common Positions

Standing

The least stable, most common "you have no choice" position — often braced against a barricade edge or post rather than freestanding. Feet roughly shoulder width, weight even, and look for any bone-on-prop contact you can get (shoulder, forearm, back of hand) rather than trying to freehand it.

Kneeling

Front knee up, rear knee down, sitting back onto the rear heel where your ankle allows it. Braced kneeling — front knee or forearm against a barricade — is far more common in NRL22 than unsupported kneeling.

Seated

Elbows in front of or on top of knees, off a barricade top or on the ground behind a low prop. Stable in the fore-aft axis, often the weak axis is left-right — check that by nudging the rifle off NPA left and right and feeling how easily it returns.

Prone off a prop

Different from prone on the ground: instead of bipod-and-ground, you're often draped over a barrel, ladder rung, or barricade top with a rear bag. Get as much of your body weight settled and consistent behind the rifle as the prop allows, rather than hovering over it.

Common Mistakes

The same handful of errors show up across every position, repeated consistently enough by coaches and instructors that they're worth naming directly:

  • Death-gripping the rifle. Tension in your hands shows up as wobble on the reticle, especially braced on a barricade — hold with the minimum grip that keeps the rifle controlled.
  • Resting the rifle bare against a hard prop. Put something soft between the rifle and a barricade edge or ledge — a bag, a gloved hand, your own forearm — rather than metal or wood on metal or wood.
  • Gripping the fore-end out of habit. On a barricade, pressing your support hand flat against the prop itself is often more stable than holding the rifle the way you would freehanded.
  • Forcing a textbook position onto a prop that doesn't fit it. Build whatever's actually stable for that specific barricade or shape, not the named position it most resembles.
  • Breaking position immediately after the shot. Stay put and call your shot before moving — you lose that feedback the instant you come off the gun.
04

Barricades, Props & Bags

Keep the barrel free

Rest the rifle's stock, chassis, or the front of a free-floated handguard on the prop — not the barrel itself. A barrel that's touching something changes how it vibrates as the bullet passes through, and that shifts point of impact in a way that's inconsistent shot to shot depending on exactly how hard it's pressed against the prop.

Rear bag manipulation

A squeeze bag under the rear of the stock, controlled with your support hand, is the standard way to make fine elevation adjustments once the front is set on the prop — squeeze to raise the muzzle, relax to lower it. It's a different skill from gripping a fore-end and takes its own practice to get smooth.

Common props you'll see

  • Barrels and barrels-on-barrels (varying height and diameter)
  • Ladders and stairs (shooting through rungs or off a step)
  • Walls, window/port cutouts, and VTAC-style multi-angle barricades
  • Tank traps and A-frames
  • Tables, tripods, and other improvised rests

The prop changes every stage; the checklist in Building A Position above doesn't. For which support to grab on which prop — bag, bipod, or both — see Bags & Bipods.

Sling Setup & Technique

There's no sling requirement in NRL22 — the rulebook doesn't restrict them, and plenty of competitors never take one out of the bag. But once you're kneeling or sitting off a barricade, a correctly-used sling removes a real amount of wobble from the reticle for free. Both classic techniques below are worth knowing even if you end up running a modern two-point sling instead of a dedicated leather one.

Loop Sling

The traditional target/service-rifle technique. It takes longer to get into than a hasty sling, but it does more real work — the sling itself, not your arm muscles, ends up carrying the rifle's weight, which is why it's the standard for sitting, kneeling, and prone.

  1. Loosen the sling's front section out of carry configuration.
  2. Slide your support arm through the loop, between the sling and the stock, then rotate the loop up and around your upper arm, above the bicep.
  3. Cinch the loop snug high on the arm — not down at the elbow — roughly where the posterior deltoid meets the triceps.
  4. Bring the rifle up into your shoulder pocket and let the sling's tension carry the weight, rather than holding it up with your arm.
  5. Expect a slightly different strap length for kneeling, sitting, and prone — re-adjust rather than forcing one length to work everywhere.

Hasty Sling

A faster, cruder version with a real speed advantage and a real stability tradeoff.

  1. Without touching any buckle or adjustment, pass your support arm between the sling and the stock from underneath.
  2. Wrap the strap once around the outside of your bicep.
  3. Pull the rifle back into your shoulder — the wrap itself creates the tension; there's no loop or cinch step.

Because there's no true loop locking the sling to your arm, most of that tension just pulls the rifle rearward rather than truly stabilizing it — the stability gain is real but modest. Its advantage is speed and that it works standing, where a loop sling doesn't help much.

Is This Worth Learning For NRL22 Specifically?

Honestly: it's a legacy service-rifle and High Power skill more than a purpose-built NRL22 one, and NRL22 gear requirements are notably relaxed — a fair number of competitors do fine with just a bag and no sling at all. Where it earns its keep is a dedicated unsupported stage — sitting or kneeling with no barricade or prop to lean on — which NRL22's own course-of-fire structure builds in most months. On barricade stages, your bag and the prop are already doing the stabilizing job a sling would otherwise do, so the payoff is smaller there. If you're still building the bag skills in Bags & Bipods, that's arguably the higher-value thing to master first — add a sling once unsupported stages start costing you points a sling would fix.

Common Mistakes

  • Looping too low, at the elbow instead of the bicep. This loses the deltoid/triceps lock the technique depends on — most of the stability benefit disappears.
  • Inconsistent tension between zeroing and match use. A sling pulls the rifle into your shoulder with real force, and shooters have reported point-of-impact shifts of close to a MOA between a bench zero and a sling-tensioned position zero.
  • Wrong attachment points on a modern two-point sling. Clipping both ends near the buttstock, or one end too far forward, makes the rifle hang awkwardly and stops it from transferring tension the way it should.
  • Never practicing the on/off transition. A sling only helps if getting in and out of it is automatic — that costs real time at the line in your first few matches if you haven't drilled it.
  • Fighting the sling instead of relaxing into it. Muscling against the sling's own tension adds wobble back in instead of removing it — the goal is to let the sling, not your arm, hold the weight.
Zero it in the position you'll actually shoot Because sling tension pulls the rifle into your shoulder with real, variable force, your zero can shift by close to a MOA between a bench zero and a sling-tensioned field zero. If you're going to use a sling in competition, confirm your zero in the position and tension you'll actually use — not just off the bench.
What to actually buy as a beginner To genuinely learn the classic technique, a simple padded loop/cuff sling is enough — no-frills, built to be used in the traditional sense in sitting, kneeling, and prone. If you'd rather have one sling that covers carry and support without a second purchase later, a configurable two-point sling built for this — Magpul's RLS is a common NRL22-specific pick, alongside PRS-style options from Rifles Only, TAB Gear, and Armageddon Gear — gets you loop-style tension without a dedicated leather sling. Either way, NRL22 gear requirements are relaxed enough that this isn't worth overthinking before your first match.

Practice

Positional skill is mostly reps, and most of those reps can happen at home dry firing against a chair, a step stool, or a cheap barricade prop — see Fundamentals → Dry Fire. Build the position, settle, check NPA, press the trigger without disturbing it — then reset and do it again.
